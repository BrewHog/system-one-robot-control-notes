Working Notes / note 08

Fast judgment and slow thought: what actually changed

On System 1 models, frontier intelligence, and the claim that reactive semantic control is newly possible.

2026-09-18 · Justin (BrewHog)

I have spent the last stretch of work trying to put a fast model inside a robot control loop. This note is where I try to say clearly what I think is genuinely new about the models that make that possible — and, at more length, what is not new, because the loose version of this argument is wrong and I would rather state the defensible version myself than have someone else dismantle the indefensible one.

The claim

Stated as carefully as I can manage:

For the first time, a robot's low-latency reactive layer can be given judgments written in natural language rather than thresholds written in code — and those judgments can be replaced at runtime without retraining the controller or changing the control law.

That is the claim I will defend. Not "reactive robots are new." Not "AI can now play video games fast." Those are old news and I will say so in detail below. The new thing is narrower and, I think, more consequential: the interface to the fast layer changed from arithmetic to language.

Two things that got confused

Two classes of model are being described as though they were one, and the confusion matters because they solve different problems in a robot.

Typed-judgment models: Jev

TypeSafe's System One models, of which Jev is the flagship, evaluate a state and return typed answers and probabilities. You define the answer space using three primitives:

PrimitiveQuestion shapeExample
Noulwhether a condition holds"is a wall close enough to matter?" → 0.95
Choiceone of a defined set"which waypoint should it head to?" → "north-east shelf"
Scoreposition on an ordered scale"how much speed should it commit?" → 2.1 of 3

The important properties are that the output is constrained by construction — code consumes it directly with no parsing and no prose to interpret — and that answers carry a confidence value, so code can decide when to act and when to escalate. These models are trained for calibrated decisions, meaning their probabilities are optimised against outcomes rather than being a side effect of generation.

The decisive property for control work is latency. In my own runs the median round trip for a three-question judgment was 146 ms (note 03), with a 12-sample spread of 112–223 ms. That is not a hard-real-time guarantee, but it is the difference between a judgment layer that can sit inside a robot's decision cycle and one that cannot.

Edge automation models: Needle 3

Cactus Compute's Needle 3 is a different animal, and I want to describe it accurately rather than fold it into the same story. It is a foundation model for tiny devices — a single 8–29 MB binary — doing three jobs entirely on-device: tool calling (pick the right function and fill every argument), structured extraction (declare a shape, get typed fields back), and text embedding.

Two design choices are worth naming:

  • A capacity ladder. One set of weights is trained so every depth from 2 to 20 layers is a deployable model in its own right. A 2-layer subnetwork and a 20-layer one are the same model at different rungs, and any rung can be fine-tuned independently.
  • Grammar-constrained decoding. A byte-level grammar compiled from your schemas constrains every token, so the output parses by construction rather than by hope. Off-topic requests return an empty list instead of a guess.

Cactus reports 400–4 k tokens/s decode on a Raspberry Pi 5, and reports that fine-tuning a 4-layer subnetwork (from 29M parameters) on a downstream task surpasses DeepSeek V4 Flash on their mobile tool-call suite. Those are their published figures on their evaluation sets, and I have not reproduced them. I cite them as claims with a provenance, not as measurements of mine.

So the latency story is different from Jev's. Jev's 146 ms is a network round trip to a hosted model. Needle's speed is a local forward pass with no network in the loop at all — a categorically different latency class, with the corresponding trade of general capacity for narrow competence.

The objection, stated at full strength

Here is the strongest version of "this is nothing new," and it deserves to be met head-on rather than waved away.

Robots have had fast reactive layers for decades. Subsumption architectures in the 1980s ran reactive behaviour on hardware slower than a modern watch. Whole-body control and model-predictive control run at hundreds of hertz to kilohertz. Learned visuomotor policies have produced genuinely reactive manipulation for years. Boston Dynamics machines do not deliberate before catching themselves. If the claim is "fast reactions," it was true in 1986.

And frontier models have been used in robotics for years. Say-Can, Code as Policies, VoxPoser and a long line of successors put an LLM in the loop to produce plans or code that a low-level controller then executes. The asymmetry between slow reasoning and fast control is the standard architecture, not a discovery.

And the models are not intelligent in the frontier sense. A Choice between four waypoints and a 4-layer classifier are not general intelligence. They cannot reason about a novel situation, explain themselves, or do anything outside the answer space you defined. Calling a constrained classifier "frontier LLM intelligence" would be simply false, and I am not going to make that claim.

All three objections are correct. I concede all three.

What is actually new

Now the narrow claim, which survives all three objections.

1. The reactive layer's policy is authored in language, not arithmetic

Every reactive robot that came before needed its reflexes written: a threshold, a gain, a cost function, a trained policy. Expressing a nuanced policy meant finding the numbers that encoded it. "Slow down when the corner is tight and the surface is slick" is a sentence a person can write and a nightmare to convert into thresholds that behave.

With a System One model, that sentence is the implementation. My wall-risk question was literally:

"Is the robot close enough to a wall that driving forward at full speed
 risks hitting it within about one second?"

and that phrasing produced usable, state-varying answers at 146 ms without my writing a single threshold. This is not "the model is smarter than MPC." Instead, the semantic gap between intent and behavior narrowed dramatically. The reactive layer inherited an LLM's ability to understand a described situation, without inheriting its latency.

2. The judgment is swappable at runtime

This is the part I find most underrated, and it is a direct consequence of the interface being language rather than weights. Changing what a classical controller does means rewriting and re-tuning code; changing what a trained policy does means collecting data and retraining. Changing what my robot does means sending different text — no deployment, no retraining, no new binary:

# the same controller, a different policy, at runtime:
"how much of the available speed should the robot use ...?"
criteria = ["crawl or hold position", "move slowly",
            "move at moderate speed", "move at full speed"]

Then swap the criteria for a cautious mode, or a mode that values smoothness over speed, and the control law consumes it unchanged. The reactivity and the control law are decoupled from the policy. I have not seen that in a reactive robot layer before, and it is the thing I would most want someone to try to falsify.

3. Confidence turns "act or ask" into a first-class control signal

A threshold produces a number. A calibrated judgment produces a number and a statement about its own reliability. Both Jev and Needle 3 return calibrated confidence, and Needle's engine implements a floor that withholds a call below it. That lets the fast layer do something new: participate honestly in its own escalation. "I am not sure enough to act on this" becomes a signal the architecture can route on — act, confirm, or hand off to a deliberative model — rather than a failure mode discovered after the robot has already moved.

This is what makes the two-tier architecture a real architecture rather than a performance trick. The boundary between System 1 and System 2 is not fixed by latency. It is negotiated, per decision, on evidence.

Where the frontier model actually goes

Worth stating plainly, because the phrase "frontier LLM intelligence" in the framing of this question is the part I would push back on: in the architecture I built, the frontier model is not in the fast loop and should not be. Its jobs are the ones that are genuinely slow:

LayerJobTimescale
System 2 (frontier LLM)plan the task, interpret the work order, recover when reality contradicts the plan, handle the case nobody anticipatedseconds
System 1 (Jev / Needle 3)per-decision semantic judgment: is this risky, is this worth a detour, what does this instruction mean here~100 ms and below
Control law (ordinary code)heading, distance, damping, arrival, limits20–1000 Hz

The intuition "LLM thinking, but fast" is the wrong mental model. The right one is: the frontier model's comprehension is what gets compressed into the fast layer, and only for the decisions narrow enough to be expressed as a typed question. Everything the frontier model does that is actually expensive — open-ended reasoning, recovering from the unexpected, knowing what to do when the situation is outside every category you defined — stays slow, and it should.

What my own experiment says, including against me

I have one data point, and I should be honest that it is a mixed one. The individual layers worked: the simulator answered, MCP drove it, and the judgment layer returned usable answers in 146 ms median. The composed loop failed — the robot spun in place and made zero net progress.

The failure was entirely mine, and it is instructive about where the risk in this architecture actually sits. I had a control law whose turn gain and hold duration disagreed by a factor of about four, so it could never enter its own fine-tracking regime. I also misread the Score scale and clamped a legitimate value of 2.1 (of 3) down to 1.0, silently flattening every urgency reading into a constant — and I first blamed the model for that, incorrectly.

Two conclusions, and the second is the uncomfortable one:

  1. The architecture localised the bug to the right layer. Because the decision log recorded every judgment next to the command it produced, I knew within one run that the fault was arithmetic in my code and not the model. That auditability is a real property of keeping judgment and actuation separate, and it is an argument for this decomposition that has nothing to do with latency.
  2. The bottleneck is not the model. Everything that went wrong was ordinary engineering that I got wrong. The exciting new component worked. The thirty-year-old part is where I failed. Anyone adopting this should expect that distribution: the semantics arrive cheap and fast, and the control engineering is still on you.

What I am not claiming

  • Not first to reactive robotics. Decades of prior art, cited above, and none of it is diminished by this.
  • Not real-time safe. A ~150 ms network judgment has network-shaped tail risk. Nothing I have done establishes anything about hard deadlines. A reflex layer that can be 223 ms late on one tick in twelve is not a safety layer.
  • Not intelligence transferred. A typed judgment is narrower than it sounds. Whether the compression of frontier comprehension into a constrained answer space retains what matters is exactly the open empirical question, and my evidence is one failed run.
  • Not tested on hardware. All of it is turtlesim. No dynamics, no sensor noise, no actuator lag, no safety surface.
  • Not my numbers for Needle 3. I have not run it. Its published figures are Cactus's, on their evaluations.

Why this might be the moment

Strip away the hype and the honest version is this. Fast reactive control has existed for forty years. Semantic understanding of a described situation has existed for a few years, but slowly and expensively. What appeared in the last year is the ability to have the second inside the timescale of the first — as a typed, calibrated, swappable judgment — and that combination had not been available before.

It is not that robots became intelligent. It is that the fast half of a robot stopped needing to be hand-coded in numbers, and can now be told what to care about in words. That is a smaller claim than "frontier intelligence at reflex speed," and I think it is the actual event.
